Finally, we reached the moment when our separate pieces started becoming one complete website.

Before the tutorial, my teammate and I combined all of our pages into one project. This sounds simple, but it was actually one of those ***“easy in theory”*** tasks. Since we had been working on different pages separately, some visual styles, spacing, and layout decisions were not consistent. One page looked slightly more polished, another page had different spacing, and some components did not feel like they belonged to the same website yet.

So we spent time reviewing the prototype together and making the visual direction more unified. We discussed the overall look, and tried to make the pages feel continuous when users move from page to page. At this stage, consistency became more important than making one individual page look impressive. A website can have a beautiful page, but if the next page feels like it came from another planet, the user experience still breaks.

In class, we tested the project along with our slides. We ran the website, clicked through all the flows, checked whether pages loaded properly, and made sure the interaction between pages made sense. This connected to the idea that testing should answer the question we actually have. The material itself also framed testing as checking both whether the code does what we said and whether users can actually get what they came for.

Accessibility is important too. This included things like whether the page structure was understandable, whether text contrast was acceptable, whether buttons and links were clear, and whether the website could be navigated without becoming confusing. I realised accessibility is not something that can just be sprinkled on at the end like decorative sugar. It needs to be checked through the actual experience of using the site. The lecture also reminded us that automated accessibility checks are useful but not enough by themselves, especially when the brief requires AA accessibility.

Another part was confirming that we had addressed the earlier requirements. We looked back at what we planned before: Sound Rooms, homepage discovery, detail pages, comments, user profiles, database structure, and interaction. This was useful because during development, it is very easy to get distracted by small bugs or nice-to-have ideas and forget the original functional requirements. Checking the requirements again helped us decide what still needed fixing and what could be left as future improvement.

Looking back at the whole development process, this project has changed a lot from where we started. At the beginning, it was all began with a pure and naive idea: an indie music community where users could discover rooms, listen, and discuss. Then it slowly became a wireflow, then HTML pages, then a database structure, then a more interactive prototype. Each week forced us to look at the same project from a different angle. This goes beyond the scope of our regular assignment, and we really want to launch it and turn it into a product because we’ve seen the demand and interest. Especially since we got some experience in the independent art scene ourselves, we’re acutely aware of the lack of such resources and the challenges faced in our country.

Getting back to the point, by the end of the week, the project felt really close to submission-ready.

It is not perfect. I can still see many things I would improve with more time. But the pages are now connected, the visual style is more consistent, the main flow works, and the requirements feel much more under control.

Anyway, we finally have something to submit now, ***without pretending too hard***.
